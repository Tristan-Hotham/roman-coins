# Cover note: Lost Money

## Suitable journals (no fee to publish; limits checked in 2026)

1. **Britannia** (Cambridge University Press, for the Society for the Promotion of Roman Studies). The author instructions suggest a guideline of 12,000 words for articles. The journal is hybrid: open access is optional and carries an article processing charge only if the author chooses Gold Open Access; the subscription route has no fee, and accepted manuscripts may be deposited in a repository. The paper's text before the references is about 7,400 words including tables and captions, so it fits. Britannia published the closest recent work (Henry 2024, the new corpus of Roman coins) and Reece's site-finds papers (1972, 1995), so the readership is right.
2. **The Numismatic Chronicle** (Royal Numismatic Society). The guidance for contributors gives no word limit, only a request for concision, and mentions no fees or charges. Submissions on Roman topics go to the Ancient editor by email. The Society asks authors to wait two years before posting the typeset article online (a typescript may go in a repository on acceptance). The paper fits as it stands.

Not suitable on cost: *Internet Archaeology* states that it is a Gold open access journal that charges a one-time article processing charge after peer review, so it does not meet the no-fee condition, although it has no length limit and has published closely related work (Walton and Moorhead 2016). Also possibly suitable, limits not checked: *Oxford Journal of Archaeology* (Wiley; subscription with optional paid open access). Check its author guidelines before submitting.

## Summary of the contribution (150 words)

Roman coin finds recorded by the Portable Antiquities Scheme are the largest body of dated material evidence for Roman England, but they are shaped by where people detect and report. This paper builds a complete district-by-period table for England from the Scheme's public search: 275,206 Roman coin records, 188,831 assigned to Reece periods, set against 1,062,032 records of every period as a measure of recording intensity. The English profile reproduces Reece's British site-find curve closely. A pre-specified test of smooth regional gradients, with the late share rising to the south and east and the early share to the north and west, finds no support under the main model, and all but one of twenty checks of the late-share test agree. In exploratory analysis, regional variation appears as a step: the North East and North West have about a quarter of the late share and four times the early share found elsewhere. The paper argues for treating the frontier zone as distinct, not for assuming a lowland gradient.
